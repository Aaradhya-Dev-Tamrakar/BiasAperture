\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{chap:litreview}

\Cref{chap:intro} described the established literature on algorithmic fairness and the engineering challenge of applying it in computer vision audits. This chapter reviews selected scientific, mathematical, and regulatory sources that inform the proposed BiasAperture architecture.

Rather than treating the literature as an uncurated catalogue of citations, we organize this review across seven coherent thematic pillars:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The Empirical Mandate}: Foundational evidence demonstrating persistent demographic and intersectional disparities in facial analysis systems;
    \item \textbf{Mathematical Formulations of Fairness}: Formal definitions of error-rate and selection-rate parities, along with critiques of borrowed legal thresholds;
    \item \textbf{Statistical Rigor and Demographic Inference Epistemology}: Uncertainty quantification, sample-size guards, and the mathematical bounds imposed by demographic inference accuracy;
    \item \textbf{Explainability Foundations and Attribution Bounds}: Cooperative game theory, additive feature attribution, and impossibility theorems bounding post-hoc explanations;
    \item \textbf{Standardized Documentation and Regulatory Governance}: Recognized reporting conventions and legal-to-technical verification mappings;
    \item \textbf{The Pipeline and Workflow Void}: The structural gap between theoretical metrics and reusable, air-gapped auditing software; and
    \item \textbf{Frontiers and Future Diagnostic Extensions}: Emerging directions in dataset-level bias profiling, contextual-intersectional stress testing, and continuous attribute partitioning.
\end{enumerate}

\Cref{tab:litreview} at the conclusion of this chapter consolidates these twenty contributing works, highlighting their specific architectural tier and operational role within BiasAperture.


\section{The Empirical Mandate for Facial Analysis Bias Auditing}
\label{sec:lit_empirical}

Buolamwini and Gebru's \emph{Gender Shades} study audited commercial gender-classification APIs across skin-type and gender intersections \cite{buolamwini2018gendershades}. They reported that aggregate accuracy masked subgroup differences: error rates ranged from 0.8\% for lighter-skinned male subjects to 34.7\% for darker-skinned female subjects. The study motivates reporting subgroup results alongside aggregate measures. The proposed BiasAperture workflow includes intersectional cohorts based on the FairFace demographic categories; this design does not imply that every combination is adequately represented or measured.

Stanley et al. studied bias encoding in convolutional networks using controlled synthetic medical images \cite{stanley2025bias}. Their work motivates caution about interpreting subgroup differences as evidence of a particular mechanism. The demographic logistic surrogate reviewed for BiasAperture uses race, gender, and age indicators to predict model outputs. It summarizes associations between those demographic indicators and predictions; it does not analyse image pixels, determine whether a visual feature is a proxy, identify confounders, or establish the cause of a disparity.

Nemavhola et al. evaluated ResNet-50, MobileNetV3, and Vision Transformer (DeiT) architectures on FairFace and UTKFace \cite{nemavhola2026reassessing}. They reported larger racial than gender disparities in their evaluated models, with reported effect sizes above \(d=1.0\). These are findings from that study, not results of the BiasAperture case study. UTKFace was excluded from BiasAperture's completed scope.


\section{Mathematical Formulations of Group Fairness and Disparity}
\label{sec:lit_fairness_math}

Hardt et al. introduced two widely used error-rate parity criteria in supervised learning: \emph{Equalized Odds} and \emph{Equal Opportunity} \cite{hardt2016equality}. Equalized Odds requires equal True Positive Rates (TPR) and equal False Positive Rates (FPR) across demographic cohorts, conditional on the true label:
\begin{equation}
    P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = a, Y = y) = P(\hat{Y} = 1 \mid A = b, Y = y), \quad \forall y \in \{0, 1\}, \forall a, b.
\end{equation}
Equal Opportunity requires parity in TPR for the outcome defined as advantageous ($Y=1$). These criteria address different questions. The proposal specifies Equalized Odds Difference (\gls{eod}) and Equal Opportunity Difference (\gls{eop}) as two of its four metrics; backend agreement is a computational cross-check, not independent evaluation.

Watkins et al. analysed the use of the U.S. Equal Employment Opportunity Commission's four-fifths rule in machine learning fairness \cite{watkins2022fourfifths}. Their discussion cautions against treating a ratio threshold as a context-free pass/fail test. The proposal accordingly specifies that Disparate Impact Ratio (\gls{dir}) and Demographic Parity Difference (\gls{dpd}) be presented with statistical context rather than as standalone threshold decisions.

Howard et al. evaluated face-recognition fairness measures against functional criteria, including interpretability, boundedness, and behavior at zero-error cases \cite{howard2022evaluating}. Their analysis illustrates that metric choice can affect comparative conclusions. BiasAperture proposes separate Fairlearn and AIF360 implementations to cross-check calculations; this does not establish independent expert assessment or validate every methodological choice. Howard et al.'s work addresses biometric verification error rates, whereas BiasAperture's proposed metrics concern classification disparities.


\section{Statistical Rigor, Uncertainty, and Demographic Inference Epistemology}
\label{sec:lit_statistics}

A point estimate alone does not describe sampling uncertainty. Nemavhola et al. paired disparity measures with bootstrap confidence intervals, analysis of variance, and Cohen's $d$ effect sizes \cite{nemavhola2026reassessing}. In the reviewed implementation, subgroup confidence intervals are empirical percentile intervals from a bootstrap routine whose default is \(B=200\); the backend adapters do not pass a larger resample budget. This falls short of the proposed NFR-002 target of at least 1{,}000 resamples. A separate global-summary routine uses a stratified BCa bootstrap, but that is not the method or budget used for subgroup rows. These implementation details should accompany any interpretation of reported intervals.

Statistical reliability depends on adequate sample support. Small subgroup samples can yield unstable estimates. The implementation applies a minimum-size guard at \(n<30\), marking such rows insufficient rather than reporting a metric value.

Demographic auditing also depends on the provenance and reliability of demographic labels. Fournier-Montgieux et al. analyse how demographic-attribute-inference errors affect fairness-audit validity and report limitations in FairFace's published training pipeline and classifier performance relative to alternative inference heads \cite{fourniermontgieux2025reliable}.

Karkkainen and Joo's FairFace dataset has 97,698 released images and seven race, two gender, and nine age categories \cite{karkkainen2021fairface}. FairFace is the retained benchmark in the completed scope; UTKFace was excluded. In line with Fournier-Montgieux et al.'s discussion of demographic-inference error, FairFace labels should be treated as benchmark annotations rather than infallible ground truth. The report should record this limitation where results are presented.


\section{Explainability Foundations and Bounds on Feature Attribution}
\label{sec:lit_xai}

Feature attribution methods draw on cooperative game theory. Lloyd Shapley established a payoff allocation for an \(n\)-player cooperative game that satisfies four axioms: \emph{Efficiency}, \emph{Symmetry}, \emph{Dummy/Null Player}, and \emph{Additivity} \cite{shapley1953value}:
\begin{equation}
    \phi_i(v) = \sum_{S \subseteq N \setminus \{i\}} \frac{|S|!(|N| - |S| - 1)!}{|N|!} \left[ v(S \cup \{i\}) - v(S) \right].
\end{equation}
Lundberg and Lee proposed SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), an additive feature-attribution framework, and derived a Shapley kernel for weighted linear regression \cite{lundberg2017unified}:
\begin{equation}
    \pi_x(z') = \frac{M - 1}{\binom{M}{|z'|} \cdot |z'| \cdot (M - |z'|)}.
\end{equation}

The reviewed surrogate implementation fits logistic regression on demographic indicator variables (race, gender, and age) to predict the audited model's predictions, then summarizes additive feature attributions. This is association-based attribution over demographic variables, not image explanation. It does not discover causes, identify proxy confounders, or decompose the measured disparity. Image-space attribution and \gls{ita} analysis are outside the current scope.

Bilodeau et al. discuss limits on feature attribution's ability to distinguish causal drivers from correlations \cite{bilodeau2022impossibility}; Slack et al. describe ways post-hoc explainers can be fooled \cite{slack2020fooling}. These findings motivate bounded interpretation. Demographic-surrogate output should be reported as association-based attribution, not as image explanation, causal discovery, proxy-confound identification, disparity decomposition, or evidence that bias is absent.


\section{Standardized Documentation, Governance, and Regulatory Context}
\label{sec:lit_governance}

A diagnostic audit benefits from clear reporting for technical and non-technical readers. The proposed report structure draws on established documentation conventions and regulatory sources.

Mitchell et al. proposed \emph{Model Cards for Model Reporting}, a documentation format for a model's intended domain, performance, evaluation datasets, and ethical considerations \cite{mitchell2019modelcards}. Gebru et al. proposed \emph{Datasheets for Datasets}, a framework for documenting dataset provenance, collection, annotation, and limitations \cite{gebru2018datasheets}. The planned HTML report is informed by these conventions and would describe the FairFace benchmark and the exclusion of UTKFace from completed scope.

Buscemi et al. discuss decomposing high-risk requirements in the European Union Artificial Intelligence Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, as amended by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) into technical verification activities \cite{buscemi2025assessing,euaiact2024,euomnibus2026}. The proposed BiasAperture crosswalk (\cref{sec:regmapping}) is a research aid, not a clause-level compliance assessment. Article 10(2)(f) concerns examining datasets for possible bias; paragraph 10(2)(g) concerns measures to detect, prevent, and mitigate biases; paragraph 10(3) addresses dataset quality and representativeness; and paragraph 10(4) addresses relevant geographical, contextual, behavioural, or functional settings. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 deleted Article 10(5) and inserted Article 4a, which provides a conditional legal basis for strictly necessary processing of special categories of personal data for bias detection and correction, subject to safeguards and conditions \cite{euomnibus2026}.

The proposed diagnostic controls can be discussed in relation to the \emph{Measure} function of the NIST AI RMF 1.0 \cite{nist2023aiframework}. Measure 2.11 addresses evaluation of fairness and bias; Measure 1.1 concerns documented approaches for measuring AI risks. Measure 1.3 calls for regular assessment involving internal experts not involved in system development or independent assessors. Fairlearn and AIF360 are software backends, not independent assessors, and their comparison does not satisfy Measure 1.3.


\section{Reusable Pipelines for Computer Vision Auditing}
\label{sec:lit_workflow_gap}

Dehdashtian et al. survey fairness and bias mitigation across the computer-vision lifecycle, including data collection, curation, annotation, training, and post-deployment monitoring \cite{dehdashtian2024survey}. Their review describes a broad literature on metrics and mitigation methods. BiasAperture addresses a narrower engineering goal: specifying a reusable workflow for subgroup evaluation of facial-analysis predictions. General-purpose fairness toolkits such as Fairlearn and AIF360 may require data preparation to apply to this setting.

The proposed BiasAperture workflow is intended to combine dataset ingestion, fairness calculations, statistical summaries, bounded demographic-surrogate attribution, and HTML reporting. It does not introduce a new fairness metric or mitigation method. Reports are intended as traceable research evidence, not compliance reports or proof of compliance. The design excludes model retraining, weight modification, and synthetic augmentation.


\section{Frontiers and Future Diagnostic Extensions}
\label{sec:lit_future_frontiers}

This section reviews three works relevant to possible extensions beyond the current scope.

First, Cascone et al. established a formal framework for evaluating representational and stereotypical bias at the dataset level, prior to and independent of model training \cite{cascone2026framework}. Auditing CelebA, LFW, and MAAD-Face, the authors demonstrated that representational disparities collapse to a single latent factor, whereas stereotypical attribute correlations vary widely across datasets. This work motivates a future \textbf{Tier 2} extension for BiasAperture: an upstream dataset-profiling module that audits training and validation corpora for representational skew and stereotypical correlation before model predictions are ingested.

Second, Aslam et al. introduced \emph{CIFA} (Contextual-Intersectional Fairness Auditing), demonstrating that traditional demographic-only audits fail to uncover severe ``hidden subgroup'' performance degradations caused by environmental and contextual factors \cite{aslam2026cifa}. Evaluating models across combined demographic and contextual axes (such as illumination variations, motion blur, and facial accessories), CIFA revealed performance drops of over 26\% in contextual subgroups that aggregate audits completely masked. While CIFA's proposed mitigation re-training is out of scope for BiasAperture's diagnostic mandate, its contextual-intersectional evaluation paradigm represents a direct candidate for future diagnostic stress testing.

Finally, Shilova et al. addressed the limitations of fixed categorical demographic schemas in their \emph{FairGroups} framework \cite{shilova2025fairgroups}. The authors demonstrated that quantizing continuous biological attributes---such as skin tone represented in continuous CIELAB color space---into coarse, predefined discrete bins (such as the 6-point Fitzpatrick scale) can obscure substantial intra-group disparities. Using data-driven partitioning, FairGroups identified vulnerable subgroups that standard discrete bins missed. For BiasAperture, whose Milestone M1 schema is locked to discrete categorical axes ($7 \times 2 \times 9$), Shilova et al.'s work provides the theoretical blueprint for a future continuous-attribute auditing module that evaluates continuous skin reflectance (ITA) alongside discrete demographic taxonomies.
